# Speaking, self-description, and signifying chains

[Home](../../README.md) · [简体中文](lacan-and-description-logic.zh-CN.md)

Someone says, “I don't want the model to decide for me.” Later they add, “But I want it to compare the options clearly and point out what I have overlooked.” The second sentence does not simply overturn the first, yet changes how we understand “deciding for myself” here: independent judgment can include help from others.

Following this imagined expression, the essay considers how speaking becomes a self-account and acquires new meaning through responses and rereading. Lacan's accounts of the subject, language, and retroaction supply philosophical questions; description logic makes selected assertions, constraints, and their consequences available for explicit examination. The [Lacan reading guide](lacan-reading-guide.md) explains the source concepts in detail; here the question is how they enter expression and personal modeling.

## Three levels of negation

“I don't want the model to decide for me” first expresses a negated content. The speaker rejects a way of collaborating, but the sentence also brings that way into discussion and asks the listener for a response.

Three questions therefore need distinction: what the sentence asserts, why it is said to this listener at this time, and how the speaker accepts or rejects the content concerned. They concern propositional truth, the situation of speaking, and the subject's attitude toward content, respectively.

If $P$ represents “I want the model to decide for me,” speaking $\neg P$ is a recordable event. Judging whether $P$ or $\neg P$ matches this person's actual wishes also requires understanding what they mean by “decide” and the situation involved. Negation makes content a topic of discussion without logically affirming its truth.

In the analytic setting, Freud's [“Negation” (1925), pp. 1–3](https://lutecium.org/ftp/Freud/pdf/1925_negation.pdf) considers how repressed content can enter consciousness through negation and distinguishes intellectual recognition from acceptance. This question draws attention to the differences among speaking, recognizing, and accepting. The form of a negative sentence alone cannot justify interpreting the example's refusal as a hidden wish.

## Enunciation and content

The statement's content (*énoncé*) concerns what is said; enunciation (*énonciation*) also concerns who speaks, from what position, to whom, and in which situation. These relations affect what a sentence does within an exchange.

The example's first sentence might respond to a choice the model made without asking, express expectations about future collaboration, or clarify the distinction between “receiving help” and “handing over the decision.” The isolated sentence does not tell us which. We need to return to what it answers and the exchange that follows.

To organize these relations, we can record $\langle\phi,p,c,t,o\rangle$: expressed content, speaking position, context, time, and the other addressed. This is a working representation; some positional relations are directly available from records, while others require interpretation.

Recording “they first said $P$, then later said $\neg P$” can faithfully preserve two acts of speaking. Whether it establishes a contradiction in content depends on the sentences' objects, conditions, and referred times. Changing one's mind, revising a word's meaning, and simultaneously accepting opposing propositions are different situations. [Forming a life trajectory](flow-and-time.md) further discusses this temporal relation.

## Self-description and formal views

Together, the two sentences can form a self-account: “I want to retain the decision, while letting the model undertake comparison and criticism.” This account connects different expressions and opens the manner of collaboration to further discussion. The participants might agree, for example, that the model presents candidates and reasons, the person makes the final choice, and the model executes it after receiving the corresponding authorization. This is one arrangement for realizing “retaining the decision” as a way of collaborating.

Description logic can explicitly examine the consequences of selected relations. The TBox represents adopted concepts and constraints; the ABox represents particular assertions. Given a consistent ontology $T$ and an assertion view $R$ consistent relative to $T$, we can write:

$$E=\operatorname{Cn}_T(R).$$

$E$ collects the consequences entailed by $T$ and $R$ in the selected language. This representation lets us check whether accepting these provisions also commits us to conclusions not directly written out.

For example, deliberately adopt the rule “participants who need to choose for themselves need comparable options,” then assert “this person needs to choose for themself.” The requirement to provide them with comparable options follows. It is a consequence of the selected rule and assertion; an actual interface allowing only acceptance of one result would fail this design requirement. The rule can also be questioned: in some situations, someone may want to delegate execution while retaining the right to change the delegation. The adopted concepts or conditions then need revision.

The formal view makes certain relations in a self-account explicit. We still need to ask whether the person accepts that account, whether it fits the current situation, and whether use brings something previously unconsidered. Formal inference and understanding a person thus raise different questions within the same work.

## Self-image and the subject in speaking

<a id="non-exhaustion-and-formal-responsibility"></a>

A self-account can also support identification. “I am an independent person” can become a way of seeing oneself, choosing actions, and responding to others. In [“The Mirror Stage” (1949), the opening discussion of identification](https://ecole-lacanienne.net/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/1949-07-17.pdf), Lacan examines how the subject acquires unity through an image and the external relations involved in that identification. The self-image itself thereby becomes an object of understanding.

Concerning the spoken “I,” [“Subversion of the Subject” (1960)](https://recherche-lacan.gnipl.fr/2015/07/05/les-ecrits-subversion-du-sujet-et-dialectique-du-desir-dans-linconscient-freudien-1960/) introduces another distinction: “I” can indicate the speaking subject, but that indication is not a semantic account of the subject. The essay also examines the subject within the relation in which one signifier represents the subject for another signifier. The non-coincidence of subject and representation here concerns a constitutive relation involving signifiers.

Returning to the example, understanding concerns more than the properties contained in the image of “independence.” It also concerns the relationship within which the person speaks it, the response they expect, and how subsequent speech changes it. The self-account can therefore be adopted, questioned, or revised. The formal view gives selected properties and rules within it explicit consequences; the two inquiries help understand the same expression from different directions.

## Signifying chains and works

“Independence” does not derive its meaning from the word alone. Following “not letting the model decide for me” with “wanting it to compare options” gives independence more specific relations to assistance, judgment, and responsibility. Later expression can thus change how an earlier word is understood.

A short notation can retain the order of expressions:

$$c_1\longrightarrow c_2\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow c_n.$$

Here $c_i$ marks an expression, and the arrows indicate succession. Understanding the passage also requires examining which words organize other expressions and which distinctions only become clear later. Order alone does not supply these semantic relations.

In his discourse construction in [*Seminar XVII*, the session of 26 November 1969](https://recherche-lacan.gnipl.fr/2013/07/23/lxvii-lenvers-de-la-psychanalyse-1969-1970-lecon-du-26-novembre-1969/), Lacan uses $S_1$ and $S_2$ to discuss the functions of the master signifier and knowledge. Understanding whether an expression has such a role requires explaining how it organizes the relevant discourse. The $c_i$ marking succession and these functional symbols answer different questions.

Works preserve expression as something that can be encountered again. Readers can compare distant passages, and authors can see where they have used one word with different meanings. Diagrams, examples, and writing can make this comparison easier, but the concrete relations they present do the work. Placing “deciding for myself” alongside “performing all the comparisons myself,” for example, can clarify the unexplained distance between them.

## The Other and retrospective meaning

The language someone uses to describe themself was not created by them alone. “Independent,” “responsible,” and “needing help” already have various uses in family, school, work, and social exchange, carrying recognition and demands. The Other concerns the locus of signifiers and speaking; particular people or institutions can bear these demands within a relationship. Lacan's [1966 radio discussion, p. 2](https://ecole-lacanienne.net/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/1966-12-02a.pdf) considers the unconscious and the discourse of the Other through the linguistic environment.

How this person uses “independence” may therefore involve the position from which they once heard the demand and whom they are now addressing in declaring their independence. The model needs to attend to these relations when organizing language. A field saying “the user prefers independence” has yet to explain what the sentence means to them.

Through the retroactive effect of discourse, [“Subversion of the Subject”](https://recherche-lacan.gnipl.fr/2015/07/05/les-ecrits-subversion-du-sujet-et-dialectique-du-desir-dans-linconscient-freudien-1960/) explains how later connections can participate in determining the meaning of earlier expression. In the example, “wanting the model to compare options” further interprets the earlier refusal. Later still, the person may discover that they sometimes want to delegate the whole choice. “Independence” then needs to be articulated anew.

Let the present interpretation of an earlier expression be written:

$$\mu(c_i\mid C,t).$$

$c_i$ is the earlier expression, $C$ the currently available interpretive context, and $t$ the time of interpretation. This notation helps retain an actual distinction: the same sentence can receive different understandings in different contexts and at different times. It locates an interpretive relation; the interpretation's content still needs to be formed from material.

Later understanding sometimes discovers a previously unclear connection and sometimes brings an understanding not previously available. What was originally said can remain preserved while its meaning in current life changes. Expression therefore does more than transmit completed ideas: someone writes an idea, reads its consequences, encounters others' responses, and thereby comes to understand what they are saying.

## Half-saying truth and continuing revision

A person need not first know their whole truth before beginning to write. What
they can initially say may be a wish, a recurring word, or an experience they
have yet to understand. Written expression becomes something they can encounter
again, answer, and revise. Later speech can develop it and change its earlier
meaning.

In the [opening of *Television* (1974)](https://lacanianworksexchange.net/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/19740131Television198740October6.pdf#page=1),
Lacan connects the impossibility of saying the whole truth with the real.
Half-saying therefore differs from information awaiting completion. Continuing
speech can bring new understanding without promising a final account that
exhausts the subject. Progressive disclosure in a Skill offers an analogy for
entering material in stages; unfolding already written material on demand and
continuing to form meaning through speech have different responsibilities.

Full speech supplies another source. In the [1953 text on the function and field
of speech and language, reproduced in these excerpts](https://www.causefreudienne.org/textes-fondamentaux/fonction-et-champ-de-la-parole-et-du-langage-en-psychanalyse/),
Lacan considers how speech reorganizes past contingencies in relation to the
subject's history and future. Its fullness concerns how the subject assumes
that history, which word counts and revision counts cannot measure. Early full
speech and the later half-saying of truth raise distinct questions. Connecting
them with sustained writing is this essay's development, not a procedure for
gradually filling a half-said truth. The [Lacan reading guide](lacan-reading-guide.md#full-speech-half-saying-and-truth)
distinguishes their source contexts.

Return to the example of independence. A further statement may emerge: “The
independence I value also includes deciding how to receive help, how to
delegate, and when to change that relationship.” It gives the earlier refusal
a new place. It may recognize a previously unarticulated connection or form an
understanding not previously available. The original words and events can
remain preserved; what changes is how they are connected, interpreted, and
assumed. The development of signifying relations becomes one line to examine
within a trajectory: which words return, how their positions change, and how
one response changes the next expression. Version order alone has yet to
explain that development.

**A philosophical Skill can become a form of speech that others can take up:
it gathers present understanding, lets it participate in life, and remains
open to retroactive revision through later speech.** The model reads it and
uses it in interpretation, judgment, and collaboration. Results, misunderstandings,
and new questions from actual collaboration return to the participants' speech.
The model can develop arguments, compare earlier versions, and identify
relations not yet explained; elegantly generated prose still needs the person's
recognition, questioning, and actual use.

Within this practice, “developing truth” concerns how new understanding forms,
how a person assumes their history anew, and how possibilities for subsequent
life change. Factual judgments still require evidence, and formal consequences
still require checking against their premises. Repeated polishing may merely
close an existing account more tightly. A revision matters through what it
makes possible to articulate, assume, and change. A work can be completed at a
particular moment while speech begins again from what happens later.

This also connects to [Forming a sinthome](writing-a-sinthome.md). Writing about
the person enters actual collaboration; its results prompt a new understanding
of that writing and may change how the participants sustain their practice.
What can change is the self-account, the understanding of relationships, and
the actual arrangement. Judging what changed requires connecting the original
expression with what happened later.
