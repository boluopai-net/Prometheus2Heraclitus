# Speaking, self-description, and signifying chains

[Home](../../README.md) · [简体中文](lacan-and-description-logic.zh-CN.md)

A person speaks before everything they mean has become clear to them. A sentence gives a relation a form that can be heard, answered, and read again; its return may alter the speaker's understanding of what they were trying to say. Speaking is therefore both expression and an event within self-formation. A person, the event of speaking, and the self-description it produces remain distinct.

[Lacan's “Subversion of the Subject” (1960)](https://www.causefreudienne.org/textes-fondamentaux/subversion-du-sujet-et-dialectique-du-desir-dans-linconscient-freudien/) supplies a vocabulary for exploring subject, speech, identification, and language. Description logic contributes a different precision: selected assertions, constraints, and their consequences can be inspected. This essay follows the movement from speaking to a connected self-account and back to interpretation. Its comparison asks how expression becomes organized, how that organization yields consequences, and how a speaker encounters the account that returns.

This develops one part of the sustaining practice proposed in [Writing a sinthome](writing-a-sinthome.md): a person expresses an unfinished relation, encounters it as a self-account, and can revise how they live with it. Examining that passage clarifies how a written account participates in the practice; formal closure alone does not constitute a sinthome.

## Three levels of negation

Denial makes three questions especially visible: what a sentence excludes as true, what its speaking brings into the encounter, and how the speaker takes up the content. They concern truth, discursive function, and acceptance respectively.

Propositional negation changes a proposition's truth conditions. Speaking a negation is also an event: someone expresses $\neg P$ in a context. A record of that event supports a claim about what was said. Assessing $P$ or $\neg P$ about the world calls for evidence about the proposition's object. Mention, rejection, and confirmation thus relate to the same content in different ways.

A negation can bring content into discussion and establish a speaker's relation to it. In this sense negation includes an affirmation of relevance: the content becomes something addressed. Logical negation still excludes $P$ when $\neg P$ is true. The apparent tension dissolves once presence in discourse and propositional truth are distinguished. Repeated denial can respond to another assertion, clarify a distinction, or serve another function; context guides the interpretation.

Freud's [“Negation” (1925)](https://lutecium.org/ftp/Freud/pdf/1925_negation.pdf) considers repressed content entering awareness through negation and distinguishes intellectual recognition from acceptance. This supplies the third question: admitting content into thought can differ from accepting it. Reading a particular denial through that distinction requires an interpretation of the encounter; its grammatical form alone supplies no finding about repression.

## Enunciation and content

An *énoncé* concerns what is stated; *énonciation* also concerns the position, address, and circumstances of stating it. A denial in the content can coexist with an expression organized around what it denies. Inquiry can examine that organization without declaring the denied proposition true.

A schematic speech record distinguishes $\langle\phi,p,c,t,o\rangle$: content, speaking position, context, time, and addressee. These elements make the situated event available for inquiry. “A speaker said $P$” and “the speaker later said $\neg P$” can be consistent event reports. Whether the contents conflict depends on their referents, conditions, and represented times. [The stream and time](flow-and-time.md) develops this distinction.

## Self-description and formal views

Once expressions are gathered into a self-description, the account can acquire coherence and implications its speaker had not separately stated. Description logic makes this relation inspectable: a view contains selected assertions, and its closure makes their formal consequences available. The person continues to live, speak, and encounter consequences beyond the current view.

A TBox expresses selected concepts, relations, and constraints; an ABox carries articulated assertions. Together they make a local description available for inference. The comparison with symbolic organization concerns which relations shape that description. Lacan's symbolic order concerns the broader conditions through which a subject enters language and intelligibility; bodily experience and the event of speaking contribute to the account from their own positions.

For a consistent ontology $T$ and a view $R$ consistent with $T$, define a formal closure:

$$E=\operatorname{Cn}_T(R).$$

This gathers consequences within the selected language. Compared with a coherent self-description, it explains how chosen material acquires a connected form. Formal closure answers which consequences follow from $T$ and $R$; knowledge of a person also depends on the account's fit, omissions, and later changes. The notation belongs to this essay's comparison.

Consider a deliberately chosen design rule: “participants who learn through making should have access to trials.” Representing that rule in $T$ and placing “this person learns through making” in $R$ yields a requirement for accessible trials in $E$. The inference can expose a consequence the author had overlooked: an environment supplying finished answers but no opportunity to try them fails the chosen rule. Yet the inference has not established that this person actually learns through making, or that every activity requires a trial. Those questions return to the person's expression and actual practice. Revising either the rule or the assertion changes the resulting view.

Lacan's [“The Mirror Stage” (1949), opening discussion of identification](https://ecole-lacanienne.net/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/1949-07-17.pdf), develops the ego's relation to image and identification; the subject appears through the relations of signification in which a stated “I” takes its place. The comparison with $E$ concerns an articulated, currently coherent self-account. That account can support identification, while the speaker can question it or discover an implication they had not intended. Formal views ask what follows from a description; psychoanalytic concepts ask how a subject is positioned through language and identification.

## Signifying chains and works

Expressions gain meaning through differences, connections, and their organization in a work. A condensed name can gather several relations, while later concepts alter how that earlier expression is understood. For an actual expression sequence, write:

$$c_1\longrightarrow c_2\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow c_n.$$

The indices locate expressions; arrows indicate their connection in the sequence. Lacan's $S_1$ and $S_2$ concern theoretical functions of master signifier and knowledge; see [*Seminar XVII*, 26 November 1969, the opening construction of discourse](https://recherche-lacan.gnipl.fr/2013/07/23/lxvii-lenvers-de-la-psychanalyse-1969-1970-lecon-du-26-novembre-1969/). To compare an expression sequence with those functions, interpretation asks how a particular expression organizes the others: what it anchors, what it permits, and how later expressions alter its force.

A work can organize words, mathematical symbols, images, color, bilingual correspondences, and notes for repeated reading. Each medium contributes through the relations it makes perceptible. A diagram can spatialize a relation that a sentence unfolds in time; a bilingual correspondence can expose a difference hidden in one formulation. Lacan's Real, Symbolic, and Imaginary concern relations in his theory, articulated in [*Seminar XXII, R.S.I.*, 10 December 1974](https://www.freud-lacan.com/documents-ged/extraits-du-seminaire-xxii-de-jlacan-rsi-2/); interpreting a work through them calls for an account of what each feature does, rather than classification by its material alone.

## The Other and retrospective meaning

The Other provides a reference for the place of language, norms, and intelligibility. Particular interlocutors or institutions convey or contest these norms from situated positions. Lacan's [1966 radio talk](https://ecole-lacanienne.net/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/1966-12-02a.pdf), especially p. 2, describes the unconscious as the discourse of the Other and as structured like a language. It also treats language as a milieu in which people develop. This theoretical account gives the essay a way to examine how already available language participates in new expression.

Prior language, encounters, experience, and new expression all participate in meaning. Learning a distinction can reorganize earlier relations and later expression: a word already used acquires a significance the speaker can now articulate. YinYang connects this movement to [Reasoning within an unfinished stream](reasoning.md), where each act of interpretation can become an event affecting what follows.

For retrospective interpretation, write:

$$\mu(c_i\mid C,t).$$

$c_i$ is an earlier expression, $C$ the available interpretive context, and $t$ the interpretation time. Changed context can change the current interpretation of the same expression. This notation locates the relation that inquiry examines. The [primary-text selection from “Subversion of the Subject”](https://www.causefreudienne.org/textes-fondamentaux/subversion-du-sujet-et-dialectique-du-desir-dans-linconscient-freudien/) supplies a reference for the subject receiving a message from the place of the Other and for retroaction.

Reinterpretation changes how the past is currently understood. What occurred remains the object of that interpretation, while its significance can enter a later self-understanding or next action differently. A spiral gives this return a form: the work is encountered again from a changed position. Sometimes the return stabilizes an understanding; sometimes it makes another beginning possible.

The work can thus answer back. An unfinished relation becomes a sentence; on the page, its implication may differ from what the author expected. Reading it later can make an earlier thought newly intelligible. The philosophical interest lies in this circuit: expression gives understanding an external form, and encountering that form supplies material for self-understanding.

## Non-exhaustion and formal responsibility

A work can represent certain relations precisely while leaving experience and subsequent changes outside its scope. Non-exhaustion permits definite local knowledge; it asks which part has been articulated and which remains unexplained. Formal rigor can establish a bounded consequence without completing an account of its subject.

[Lacan's divided subject](https://www.causefreudienne.org/textes-fondamentaux/subversion-du-sujet-et-dialectique-du-desir-dans-linconscient-freudien/) raises a structural question about signifying representation: the subject of speaking and its representation remain in a relation of non-coincidence. Multiple logical views and revisable self-understanding exhibit their own forms of non-coincidence. Comparing them asks what each view articulates and what movement the comparison makes intelligible. Definitions and derivations establish formal consequences; interpretation develops the philosophical relation; evidence assesses an account of a particular person. Their distinct responsibilities let local rigor contribute to an open-ended understanding.
